# Making a new poem

The square is a constraint, not the subject. Aim for five lines that move through a scene, thought, or exchange in every supported reading. See the [collection](../more_poems.md) for examples and the [editing guide](editing.md) for tools.

## Choose a form

Use five rows of five simplified Chinese characters. Punctuation sits outside the grid; written 儿 occupies a cell even when pronounced as erhua.

| Form | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Rhyming pair | → and ↓ form different poems; all five lines rhyme in each. |
| Symmetric square | → and ↓ form the same poem: the grid equals its transpose. |
| Omnidirectional poem | All → ↓ ← ↑ readings work; neither rhyme nor twenty distinct lines is required. |
| Unrhymed pair | → and ↓ form different poems, without a rhyme requirement. |

Read rows in top-to-bottom order and columns in left-to-right order. Arrows change the direction *within* each line. Diagonals and arbitrary paths are not part of these forms.

## Draft several ways

For rhyming pairs, the right edge supplies the horizontal endings and the bottom edge supplies the vertical endings. Their shared corner forces a shared rhyme family when every line rhymes under one consistent classification. Try meaningful five-character borders first, then fill the crossings. Check pronunciation in context, not just spelling; our [rhyme reference](../poems/rhymes.json) uses modern Mandarin families, without classical tone-pattern requirements.

Give independent writers different briefs before showing them existing grids:

- **A physical scene:** begin with rhyming borders that describe something observable. Let both readings move through the same place differently.
- **A human exchange:** use ordinary speech and one concrete gesture. Make it clear who speaks, acts, or waits in each reading.
- **A change of meaning:** rearrange agency, departure and return, or action and difficulty. Seek a consequential difference, not merely different nouns.
- **Symmetry:** separately explore mutual speech or a developing thought. Each new row also fixes a column; revise them together.

Keep promising partial grids. Change one crossing at a time and reread both affected lines, then both whole poems. For four-direction work, inspect the reverse readings from the start.

## Review before polishing

Have someone other than the writer read each direction as an independent five-line poem before explaining the construction. Judge the weaker reading first: fluency, clear agency, progression, specificity, and what the change of direction contributes. Shared lines are acceptable; counting distinct lines is not a quality score.

Scripts check dimensions, computed readings, symmetry, punctuation preservation, declared rhyme data, and translation coverage. They cannot establish natural Chinese, a defensible pronunciation in this sentence, or poetic coherence. Read aloud and verify unfamiliar or ambiguous endings. Do not supply an invented backstory to rescue a strained line.

Translate only after the Chinese settles. Preserve pronouns, ambiguity, and meaningful differences; do not force English rhyme or conceal weak Chinese with a smoother invented meaning.

## Lessons worth keeping

- **[留饮](../more_poems.md#留饮):** replacing 且 with 火 improved both 灯火与君留 and 风寒火暖酒. A good repair strengthens both crossings through a concrete image.
- **[姐姐](../more_poems.md#姐姐):** 错→够 repaired the complaint, but did not repair the rhyme. Intended *me* for interrogative 么 is not a robust ordinary-pronunciation pass; its conditional status remains explicit.
- **[未完的对话](../more_poems.md#未完的对话):** twenty distinct lines produced strained sequences of commands. More readings did not make a stronger poem.
- **[寄知己](../more_poems.md#寄知己):** individually grammatical lines left the holder, writer, and sender unclear. Whole-poem coherence needs its own review.
